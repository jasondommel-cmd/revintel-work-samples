Signal-Weighted ICP Scoring

Account fit as an explicit allocation of 100 points across seven signals in three classes — Fit (can we serve them), Opportunity (how much value is there), and Timing (why now) — with separate weighting profiles per segment, a hard viability gate, and an activation layer that pushes every score into the CRM for Sales, Marketing, and Customer Success. From score to CRM — where the model earns its keep

A score that lives in a spreadsheet is analysis. A score that lives on the Account record is infrastructure. The model writes to the CRM nightly, and each go-to-market team consumes the same fields through its own playbook — one number, three motions, zero debates about whose list is right.

Why the model is built this way

Signals are grouped by the question they answer. Fit asks whether you can serve the account, Opportunity asks how much value is there, Timing asks why now. Grouping keeps the weight debate honest — you argue about questions, not just numbers.

One dominant signal, declared openly. Most ICP models spread weight evenly to feel "balanced." If one factor gates deal viability — here, system fit — the model should say so with its weights, not hide it. And where a signal disqualifies rather than ranks, it gets a gate, not a weight.

Segment-specific profiles. Mid-market and enterprise buyers don't value the same signals equally, so each segment carries its own 100-point allocation rather than one blended formula.

Scores are only as good as the data under them. Every account carries a data-coverage figure. A 78 built on 60% coverage is a hypothesis, not a rank — and the CRM field makes that visible to every consumer of the score.

Activation is the deliverable. The model syncs to the CRM nightly and each team runs its own playbook off the same fields. Scoring work that never reaches Sales, Marketing, and Customer Success isn't a model — it's a hobby.
