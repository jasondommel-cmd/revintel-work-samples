Rule One: I Beat the Agent. That Wasn’t the Point.

Production Salesforce org

One build, run twice, is the cheapest experiment in this whole piece — and it produced the rule everything else hangs on. The build: a trade-show attribution system. Two custom objects, roughly thirty properties, the automations, the reporting. I had done this kind of work in this org before, so I knew the landmines. I built it by hand, then ran the same build with an AI agent and watched every step.

Same build. Same org. Same scope.

Observed, start to finish · agent proposed configuration, no unapproved writes to production
BY HAND
5 hrs
WITH AGENT
15 hrs
The agent was not wrong, and it was not incapable. It was thorough — pausing at every step to validate the environment and determine where to go next. I skipped that entirely, because I already knew where to go. The ten-hour gap wasn't a capability gap. It was the cost of discovery I had already paid.

Worth stating plainly: I kept the constraint that made it slower. The agent proposed configuration and never wrote to production without my approval. Speed recovered from an agent with write access to a live org is borrowed against an incident nobody has had yet.

The obvious conclusion — AI can't architect a CRM — is the wrong one. Invert the conditions and the same exhaustive behavior becomes the reason to use it. In an org where the flows are undocumented and nobody left a diagram, turning over every stone is not waste. It's the job.

So the deciding variable isn't the task. It's whether verification is redundant with knowledge already in the room. That question has a second half, because some work is judgment-dense no matter who holds the context. Put both on axes and you get a placement rule.

Rule Three: The Archive Is the Asset

Open any file

Nearly every company that owns conversation intelligence uses it to spot-check reps. The recordings are something else: a longitudinal record of what buyers actually said, the largest qualitative dataset most revenue organizations own, and the one they almost never query.

I treated 31,000+ transcripts as a research corpus and built the pipeline to query it — Databricks and Claude for pattern analysis at scale, with cluster analysis on targeted call sets. Bottom-left quadrant: nobody held that pattern, and the work had been rationed by analyst-hours since the day the tool was bought. Everything below came out of that one asset.

Delivered to Customer Support as evidence, not as a dashboard. The argument was auditable: every risk signal traced to a moment in a recorded conversation.
Where it stands: in evaluation, with a good part of it committed for adoption. Then my role and the Chief Customer Officer's were both eliminated in a restructuring. I have no outcome number and I won't borrow one.
Pattern mining across the corpus isolated what recurred in deals that progressed. Plays were written to the pattern, not to a framework someone liked.
Why it matters: a play grounded in what actually happened survives the first rep who says "that's not how my deals go."
A closed-lost field tells you a deal died. It rarely tells you why in a way anyone can act on. Structured loss analysis with call evidence turns a category into an argument you can take to product or pricing.
In the record: 79 opportunities carry structured AI lost-reason analysis with timestamped evidence.
The platform capped trackers at twenty. Rather than spend all twenty on competitor names, I reserved slots for the questions that actually needed answering and let the theme spotters carry the rest.
The general point: most AI tooling has a constraint like this. The work is designing inside it, not complaining about it.
Intelligence that lives in a slide gets read once. This went into the opportunity object, where reps and managers already work.
Reproducible: these counts come straight from the opportunity history export and can be re-run against the same data.

Rule Four: One Canon, Everyone Queries It

Governed, read-only, multiplayer

Most AI enablement means handing fifty people a chatbot. Fifty private prompts, fifty different answers, and no way to tell which one is right — so the org ends up less aligned than before it had AI. I built the opposite.

I assembled the company's canonical history into one governed library, then published it as a set of locked AI projects. Users could query them. Users could not edit them. That constraint is the entire design: the moment a rep can alter the project, the canon drifts and you are back to fifty answers.

Read-only is not a limitation here. It is the feature. One source, many questions, the same answer for everyone who asks.

The result was a learning center rather than a tool. It eliminated conjecture. Questions that used to be settled by whoever spoke with the most confidence — why we lose to that competitor, whether this account has had trouble before, what actually happens after a customer goes live — became lookups against a shared record.

This is the principle underneath both this rule and the transcript work: build a governed environment on trusted data, then broadcast intelligence that can't be found anywhere else in the company. The AI is the interface. The canon is the asset, and the governance is what makes anyone trust the answer.

Rule Five: Coverage, Not Cleverness

Coverage, not cleverness

The gain is almost never that AI decides better. It's that everything gets inspected instead of the fraction a person had time for, and human attention moves to the calls that need a judgment.

The research ceiling

Trailing quarters ran 8, 13, 4 and 10. April and May 2026 produced 71.
What I own: the input. The execution belonged to an outsourced BDR team whose capacity may also have changed in the same window, so I can't separate the two and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. The list is mine; the quarter isn't.
Agents amplify whatever data quality already exists. Hygiene and suppression came before the intelligence work, not after it embarrassed someone.
Where it stands: in production and being worked right now. I left before the results landed, so there is no outcome number here.
What changed isn't time saved. It's that a tradeoff disappeared. At ninety minutes, reps chose between something generic today and something good next week. At fifteen, the choice is gone: it goes out while the call is still warm, tailored every time.
Provenance: the ninety-minute baseline is what AEs told me it took to do it properly. Not instrumented.
Run alongside rep pipeline as a control, it surfaced unsupported risk before board review rather than after.
Across engagements: a 24% average lift in forecast predictability over seven PE-backed engagements, $10M to $300M+ ARR. That one is a cross-engagement average, not a single-company result.

Rule Six: Label What You Can’t Claim

The other half of the discipline

Deal desk stays human. Approvals and validations carry an enormous amount of context and subjectivity. AI is genuinely good at assembling an answer from disparate sources, and that is not the same as making the right call in a sensitive situation. The thoroughness that makes an agent valuable in research is exactly the wrong instinct at an approval gate, where the judgment is about relationship, precedent and consequence rather than completeness.

Two builds are in production without me. The 3,000-prospect universe is being worked right now. The churn model was in evaluation with partial adoption committed when the restructuring hit. Both are real; neither has an outcome I get to put a number on.

Attribution has limits and I mark them. The outbound jump is tied to a list I built, worked by a team I didn't manage. The research ceiling is my observation of how AEs worked, not an instrumented metric. The collateral figure is what reps told me. Every one of those is useful; none of them is the same kind of fact.

Most AI claims in a hiring process are someone else's outcome with a first-person verb in front of them. The labels on this page exist because the distinction is the whole point — and because a claim you can defend under questioning is worth more than a bigger one you can't.

The Takeaways

01

Allocation is capacity planning

Not "can AI do this" but "who is fastest, and what runs in parallel." An agent's thoroughness is waste where the knowledge is already in the room and insurance where nobody holds the map. I have the timing data: five hours against fifteen.

02

Governed data beats clever prompts

I build governed environments on trusted data and broadcast intelligence the company can't get anywhere else. 31,000 transcripts read as a corpus. Four canonical sources published as locked, queryable projects. Not fifty private chatbots — one canon, everyone querying it.

03

Label what you can’t claim

Two of the best things I built are in production without me, and I say so. Governance, provenance and honest attribution aren't the disclaimer on AI work. They're the reason anyone should believe the rest of it.
