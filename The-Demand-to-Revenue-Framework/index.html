THE ANALOGY

One Puzzle, Two Teams

Sales and Marketing are working on the same puzzle. The finished picture is the revenue outcome the business is trying to create. Every individual piece is a single prospect.

The TAM is the full box of puzzle pieces, including pieces that may not belong in our specific picture. ICP defines which pieces actually belong. From there, segmentation helps sort those pieces by type, priority, timing and ownership.

Marketing brings the right pieces to the table. Sales assembles them into the finished picture. RevOps makes sure both teams are working from the same image, using the same definitions, and following the same rules.

When something does not fit, the answer should not be Sales blaming Marketing or Marketing blaming Sales. We trace the piece backward: was it truly ICP? Was it segmented correctly? Was it introduced at the right time? Was it routed to the right seller? Was it qualified and handled appropriately?

That turns functional disagreement into a diagnostic problem. When Sales and Marketing agree on the picture they are building and the pieces that belong in it, shared accountability and collaboration become much more natural.

MARKETING

Brings the Right Pieces to the Table

Marketing’s job in this model is not volume for its own sake. It is generating demand that matches the picture being built — pieces that belong in this puzzle, not any puzzle.

Generate demand
Create awareness and interest against the agreed ICP.
Build awareness
Reach the right accounts with the right message before they are in-market.
Identify & engage

Marketing owns volume and quality. Both are measurable, both are theirs, and neither is a matter of opinion once the definitions exist.

SALES

Assembles the Pieces Into Revenue

Sales takes the pieces that arrive and builds the picture. The obligation that comes with that is honest handling — accepting inside the SLA, working what was routed, and recording what actually happened.

Engage
Accept inside the agreed SLA rather than cherry-picking the queue.
Qualify
Apply shared criteria, so a disqualification means the same thing every time.
Progress
Advance against defined stage exit criteria, not rep confidence.
Close
Win, record why, and feed that back into ICP and messaging.

Sales owns conversion, progression and revenue. When a piece doesn’t fit, the record of how it was handled is part of the diagnosis — which is precisely why the handling has to be recorded.

REVOPS

The System That Connects Both Sides

RevOps does not own demand and does not carry the number. It owns the system both teams operate inside — the definitions, the routing, the instrumentation, and the meeting where the truth gets said out loud.

Define
One set of definitions for lead, accepted, qualified, opportunity, pipeline and won.
Route
Put every piece with the right owner, by segment, territory and capacity.
Measure
Conversion and cycle time between every stage, so a gap has a location.
Analyze
Read wins, losses and stalls back into ICP, segmentation and messaging.
Diagnose
Trace pieces that didn’t fit, and name the step that failed rather than the team.
Optimize
Change the system, then verify the change moved the metric it was meant to move.

RevOps also owns two things outright, and both sit at the seams: the hand-off SLA and pipeline integrity. Neither function can be neutral about those, which is exactly why a third party has to hold them.

SUPPORTING DETAIL

The Shared Funnel

The puzzle is the agreement. This is the instrumentation underneath it — how a piece moves once it’s on the table. Every stage gets one definition, one diagnostic question, one owner and one review.

Conversion percentage and cycle time are measured into and out of every stage. A stage without both a definition and an owner cannot be diagnosed — it can only be argued about.

SORTING TRAY

ICP — Which Pieces Belong?

TAM is every piece in the box — every company that could theoretically buy. It is a sizing number and a boundary, and useful for exactly that. What it is not is a target list.

ICP is the first sort, and the one every other sort inherits. It separates the pieces that belong in this picture from the rest of the box: firmographic fit, technographic signals, segment, and the disqualifiers.

What it defines
Fit, signal, segment — and just as importantly, the disqualifiers.
Who owns it
Defined jointly by Marketing and Sales; enforced by RevOps so it can’t drift quietly.
How it changes
Deliberately, in writing, with both teams present — never by one team loosening a filter.

Get this wrong and nothing downstream can be diagnosed, because every later failure has a plausible alternative explanation. Get it right and most quality arguments simply stop.

The disqualifiers are usually the part nobody writes down. Most “lead quality” arguments are really an unwritten disqualifier that one team assumed and the other never agreed to.

SORTING TRAY

Segment — How Do We Categorize?

Not every qualifying piece belongs in the same part of the picture. Segmentation sorts by size, motion, vertical and complexity so the right play gets applied.

Why it matters downstream: an enterprise piece worked with a mid-market motion looks exactly like a conversion failure. It isn’t. It’s a sorting failure, and it will be misdiagnosed every time unless the segment is on the record.

SORTING TRAY

Priority — Which Pieces First?

Capacity is finite. Priority decides which qualifying, correctly-sorted pieces get worked now and which wait — scoring, intent strength, account tier and existing coverage all feed it.

Unstated priority is still priority. If nobody sets it, reps set it themselves one account at a time, and the aggregate is invisible to everyone. Making it explicit is what lets you ask later whether the ordering was right.

SORTING TRAY

Timing — When Do They Come In?

A perfect-fit piece introduced at the wrong moment fails exactly like a bad-fit piece, and the funnel records both identically. Timing is what separates them after the fact.

The signal question: was there genuine intent or a triggering event, or did we push the piece forward to hit a stage-conversion target? The second is common, invisible in aggregate, and resurfaces later as a stalled-pipeline problem.

SORTING TRAY

Owner — Who Is the Right Owner?

Routing puts the piece with a specific person, and that person is accountable for what happens next. Territory, segment, coverage and capacity decide where it lands.

Every stage has exactly one owner. Marketing owns volume and quality; RevOps owns the SLA and pipeline integrity; Sales owns conversion, progression and revenue. Shared ownership of a stage means nobody owns it.
