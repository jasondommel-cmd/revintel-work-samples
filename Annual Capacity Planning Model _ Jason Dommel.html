Every September this healthcare wearables company set a new-bookings goal for the following fiscal year. The sales side of capacity planning was arithmetic they already trusted: revenue goal, average order size, productivity per seller, headcount.

The support side was not. Customer support carried a heavy pre-sale and post-sale load in this business — RFP construction, contracting, implementation, onboarding — and the number of reps required to carry it had never been derived from anything. It was estimated year over year and adjusted by feel.

That is an expensive way to be wrong in either direction. Overshoot and you carry salary against work that doesn't exist. Undershoot and RFPs sit in a queue while sellers miss submission deadlines, which surfaces as a pipeline problem months later and gets blamed on the sales team.

The request was narrow, and I'd argue it was the right one: give us a concise, defensible support headcount tied to the revenue plan, and let us change our own assumptions without rebuilding the model.

02

The Method

You cannot model a function you haven't documented. Before any math, I inventoried every motion a CSR performed in support of their AE counterpart — from the moment an RFP arrived to a live, onboarded customer — then timed them.

STEP 1
Shadow the work
Traced the actual path of an RFP, not the documented one.
STEP 2
Inventory motions
36 discrete motions across seven phases, each with a clear start and finish.
STEP 3
Time each one
Time study per motion, normalized to 30-minute increments.
STEP 4
Split by outcome
Pre-decision work hits every RFP. Contracting and implementation only on wins.
STEP 5
Make it editable
Every assumption exposed, so leaders argue with the model instead of the answer.

The 30-minute increment was deliberate. Finer granularity implies a precision time studies don't support, and it invites arguments about whether a task takes 18 minutes or 22. Half-hour blocks kept the conversation on the shape of the work rather than the stopwatch.

The other design requirement was durability. Routing tools, answer-bank automation and AI drafting were all arriving. A model that assumed today's motion times would be wrong within a year — so every motion had to be adjustable, and removable. Strike any motion in the workbench and watch the headcount move.

How the math runs: goal ÷ average order size gives deals to win. Wins ÷ win rate gives RFPs to respond to. Total hours = (RFPs × pre-decision hours) + (wins × win-only hours). FTEs = total hours ÷ (2,080 × productive utilization).

03

How to Read It

It is a load model, not a productivity target. It says how many hours the plan demands and how many humans that implies. It does not tell you a rep is slow, and it should never be pointed at an individual.

Losses are the expensive part. At a 30% win rate, seven of every ten responses consume the full pre-decision load and return nothing. That's the finding that tends to change the conversation: support headcount is driven as much by bid discipline as by bookings.

Utilization is the assumption most models get wrong. 2,080 hours is not a working year. The slider sets the share actually available after PTO, holidays, training, meetings and administrative time.

Every assumption is exposed on purpose. A capacity number a CFO can't take apart is a number they will overrule. When leaders move the inputs themselves, the debate moves off the answer and onto the assumptions — the only debate worth having.

04

Takeaways

01

I replaced a guess with a derivation

Support headcount had been estimated annually with nothing underneath it. Documenting and timing 36 motions turned it into a number tied directly to the revenue plan — and one that survives being questioned.

02

The model had to outlive its assumptions

Automation and AI were going to compress these motions. So every motion is adjustable and removable. Planners test next year's tooling against this year's plan instead of waiting to find out.

03

It surfaced a finding nobody asked for

Because losing RFPs costs nearly as much support load as winning them, win rate moves headcount harder than bookings do. Capacity planning became an argument for qualification discipline.
