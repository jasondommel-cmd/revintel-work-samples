01

The Premise

Win/loss usually lives as a quarterly slide: a few anecdotes, a competitor everyone already blames, and a "we need to sell higher" bullet that changes nothing. I wanted the version that changes how the team sells on Monday.

A win/loss analysis that ends in a slide is trivia. One that ends in a qualification rule is an operating system.
The framing that drove the work

The company had years of closed opportunities sitting in Salesforce — every one a graded outcome with a buyer, a deal size, a stage history, and a documented reason for the result. That's a training set. The job was to interrogate it hard enough to produce rules a CRO could defend to a board and a rep could act on without a data dictionary.

I ran it in two passes with different jobs: a large descriptive study to map the anatomy of wins and losses, and a smaller statistical study to prove which patterns were real signal versus sampling noise. Description shows you where to look; significance testing tells you what you're allowed to build a rule on.

02

Two Datasets, Two Jobs

Breadth, then rigor

Neither study alone is enough. A big descriptive sample surfaces rich patterns but can mistake correlation for cause. A tightly controlled sample can prove a signal but can't map the whole terrain. Run in sequence, they cover each other's blind spots.

Study A · Breadth

The Buyer-Anatomy Study

777 closed opportunities · 400 won / 377 lost · primary contacts

A descriptive deep-read of who was in the room and how the deal ended — win rate by buyer seniority, by seniority × segment, by deal size, and by documented loss reason. This is the map: it shows which buyer profiles win and which quietly bleed out.

Its job: find every pattern worth testing.
Study B · Rigor

The Significance Study

88 closed deals · 44 won / 44 lost · Fisher exact tests

A balanced, controlled sample built to answer one question per test: is this pattern real? Motion split (new logo vs. expansion), the seniority gate, and the reliability of CRM fields all went through formal significance testing before any of them earned a place in a rule.

Its job: decide what's provable enough to ship.

The discipline that mattered: a pattern from Study A was never promoted to a rule until Study B could show it wasn't noise. That's the difference between "the data suggests" and "the data proves" — and it's the difference a board will notice.

The trap the average hides. Blended, "Director" looks like a fine contact to have. Split by segment and Director @ Enterprise wins just 36% — the single worst profile in the book. Directors at large orgs evaluate; they rarely control budget. A deal anchored on one is a false-champion deal.

The counterintuitive green flag. Manager @ Enterprise wins 58% — better than Directors or Presidents in the same segment. When an enterprise Manager is your primary, there's almost always an executive sponsor above them. The play isn't to distrust the Manager; it's to find and name that sponsor.

06

From Findings to Operating Rules

The output that mattered

This is where win/loss stops being analysis and becomes infrastructure. Each rule below traces directly to a finding above — and each one shipped into the way the team qualifies, forecasts, and sells.

Gate new logo on buyer seniority — not on activity
The significance study showed VP+/C-level presence is the single provable predictor of a new-logo win. It became a hard qualification gate: a new-logo deal doesn't enter committed forecast without buying authority in the room.
Evidence: Fisher exact p=0.0016 · +44pt win-rate separation
Never gate expansion the way you gate new logo
Expansion wins 78%; new logo wins 31%. A universal gate tested at p=1.00 on expansion — meaningless there. So the gate is motion-specific by design, protecting the healthiest revenue motion from friction it doesn't need.
Evidence: 78% vs 31% win rate across the closed set
Treat Director@ENT as a risk flag, not a champion
The worst profile in the book (36%). Deals anchored on an enterprise Director trigger a mandatory "find the economic buyer" play before they can advance — the single most common false-champion trap, now caught by rule.
Evidence: Director@ENT 36% vs Manager@ENT 58%
Attack "went quiet" with multi-threading, upstream
The dominant loss shape (≈45%) is disengagement, not defeat. The countermeasure is structural: a minimum-contacts requirement and an executive-sponsor play enforced early, when a deal can still be saved — not diagnosed at close.
Evidence: "Went Quiet / Can't Engage Committee" = top loss reason
Resource and forecast big deals for their real odds
Win rate inverts with deal size. Large pursuits get senior air cover and a discounted forecast weighting that matches their true close rate, instead of a face-value number that quietly inflates the pipeline.
Evidence: 81% win <$20K → 35% win >$75K
Distrust the fields that flatter you
SPICED/methodology fields were anti-predictive — losses were documented more thoroughly than wins. They were pulled from scoring and kept only as coaching hygiene, so the model reads deal reality, not paperwork diligence.
Evidence: methodology-field completion higher on losses than wins

What this demonstrates

Win/loss isn't a report you read. It's a system you run.

Most revenue teams already own the data to know why they win and lose — they just never convert it into anything that changes behavior. This work closes that loop end to end: from raw Salesforce exports, through descriptive and statistical analysis, to a set of qualification gates, forecast weightings, and sales plays the field actually runs. It's the combination a CRO rarely finds in one hire — the analytical depth to find the truth in the data, the statistical discipline to know which findings are real, and the operational instinct to turn them into rules a sales team will follow.
